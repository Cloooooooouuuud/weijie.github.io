I am a senior at Cornell studying computer science and mathematics. I study how AI systems make decisions over time: when a language model should continue, revise an earlier choice, or respond to a user's correction.

My work combines controlled evaluation with learning methods. Recent projects study decision backtracking in language models, learn writing preferences from lightweight feedback, and test whether reasoning metrics measure the behavior they claim to measure. I am especially interested in systems whose decisions can be inspected, corrected, and improved through interaction.

At Cornell, I work with Thorsten Joachims, Yoav Artzi, and Daniel Halpern-Leistner.

Research interests

I am interested in three connected problems: how models revise earlier decisions and decide how long to reason; how users can steer model behavior and teach preferences through lightweight feedback; and how evaluations can distinguish genuine improvement from artifacts of the metric or benchmark.

01

Reasoning and revision

When should a model continue, stop, or change an earlier decision? I study these choices with tasks where the relevant state or optimum can be checked directly.

02

Human feedback and steerability

How can a system learn what a user wants from ordinary language, accept/reject decisions, and comparisons, then carry that preference to later tasks?

03

Reliable evaluation

I test whether a metric measures the behavior it claims to measure, with controls for extraction error, perturbation choice, and simple baselines.

Human feedback · Thorsten Joachims Group

Interactive editing with learned writing preferences

I am building a writing assistant that learns a user's editing preferences from accepted, rejected, comparative, and user-written edits, then applies the learned preferences to new drafts.

  • Represent preferences with interpretable editing criteria and adjustable weights.
  • Combine accept/reject feedback with comparisons between candidate edits.
  • In a simulation with 120 users and 19,200 test choices, held-out choice prediction improved from 46.4% to 73.3%.
  • Use Qwen for candidate generation and evaluation and Gemma for profile-based user simulation on a GPU cluster.
  • Current analysis focuses on edit-scoring errors and evaluator order sensitivity.

Reasoning · Cornell Math+AI Lab

Latent reasoning with learned stopping

I train small recurrent models that solve algorithmic problems by repeatedly refining a hidden state and learning when to stop, rather than producing an explicit reasoning trace at every step.

  • Tasks include Sudoku, modular arithmetic, and CLRS graph algorithms.
  • Built unique-solution Sudoku data with logical-depth labels and controlled-diameter graph families.
  • Changing shortest-path and MST output representation from a harder structured target to per-node scalar regression raised accuracy from roughly 20–50% to above 90%.
  • Sudoku diagnostics trace the gap between high cell accuracy and low full-solution accuracy to unstable last-cell errors rather than simple optimization failure.
  • The experimental pipeline uses fixed seeds, paired bootstrap, immutable checkpoints, configuration hashing, and automated tests.

Interactive agents · ARC-AGI-3

Learning rules from action feedback

I build agents that discover the rules of unfamiliar interactive games from visual observations and action feedback alone.

  • Extended an open-source Qwen-based harness with visual-first interaction, image retention across turns, pixel inspection, and single-step actions.
  • Added stall detection and context-reset restarts that preserve what the agent has learned, plus a rule-based fallback explorer.
  • Matched a strong baseline score (1.31 vs. 1.35) with about 70% fewer actions.
  • Built trajectory-analysis tools that separate perception, reasoning, and execution failures and reduced scripted replay time by 32.7%.
  • Reached the top 20 on the ARC-AGI-3 leaderboard in August 2026.
